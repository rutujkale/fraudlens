## 1. Why is fraud detection different from ordinary classification?

Fraud is a rare, adversarial, non-stationary positive class: on the test window
prevalence is {{composition.prevalence:.5f}} ({{composition.frauds}} frauds in
{{composition.rows}} rows) `[final_results.json:composition]`. Because the class is
rare and the cost of a miss is asymmetric, accuracy is useless as a headline — a
model that approves everything is 99% accurate. The positives are also generated by
an adversary who adapts to the controls, so the input→label relationship moves over
time. And labels arrive late (chargebacks), so a model cannot use any row whose
label had not matured. FraudLens therefore evaluates with PR-AUC and expected cost,
splits strictly in time, and enforces a label-maturity rule
`[final_results.json:meta; docs/methodology.md]`.

**Follow-up: so what is the single number you would quote?** Frozen XGBoost test
PR-AUC {{headline.model.xgb_ci}} with the caveat that the CI is wide with
{{composition.frauds}} frauds `[final_results.json:model_comparison[2]]`.

## 2. Why PR-AUC instead of accuracy?

Accuracy is dominated by the negatives, so it hides the only thing that matters
here — ranking the few positives. PR-AUC (average precision) focuses on the
positive class and does not reward a model for the huge true-negative count. The
rules baseline is {{headline.model.rules_ci}} and XGBoost is
{{headline.model.xgb_ci}}, a gap that is invisible in accuracy
`[final_results.json:model_comparison]`. I still report accuracy, but only as a
footnote, and I report recall at fixed budgets (recall@1% =
{{headline.model.recall_at_1pct}}) because that maps to a real operating point.
`[docs/final_results.md]`

**Follow-up: why not ROC-AUC?** ROC-AUC is optimistic under imbalance; the test
ROC-AUC is high but PR-AUC is the honest primary metric
`[final_results.json:model_comparison]`.

## 3. Why was a random split inappropriate, and what is the embargo for?

A random split lets the model train on rows from the future and test on rows from
the past, which leaks time-correlated signal and overstates performance. FraudLens
splits chronologically on the time axis, not on row counts, and places an **embargo**
equal to the maximum label delay ({{freeze.split_boundaries.embargo_days}} days,
`[freeze.json:split_boundaries]`) after each cut so a row near a boundary cannot
train on a label that only matured later. The boundaries are
train→{{freeze.split_boundaries.t_train_end}}, val→{{freeze.split_boundaries.t_val_end}}
`[freeze.json:split_boundaries]`. This is why the split logic is a single tested
function `evaluation/splits.py`.

**Follow-up: how do you know the embargo matters?** In the drift lab, retraining
with point-in-time matured labels (only rows with `label_available_at ≤ step`) is a
different, smaller set than the naive one — the code enforces maturity rather than
assuming it `[docs/experiments/8E_drift.md]`.

## 4. How did you prevent and test for leakage?

Every behavioural feature for a transaction at time `T` uses only rows with
`timestamp < T`, and all window arithmetic goes through one function
(`features/windows.window_bounds`) so the leakage canary can break a single
injection point and prove the suite catches it. The suite proves truncation
invariance, future-mutation invariance, current-row and same-timestamp exclusion,
label independence, and training/serving parity, and a test asserts that
`np.searchsorted` appears in exactly one module
`[tests/test_leakage.py; docs/methodology.md]`. A registry guard
(`assert_no_target_columns`) refuses any frame that carries a label column. Finally
the canary test deliberately makes the window leaky and asserts the right tests
fail — a suite that cannot fail is not evidence.

**Follow-up: what did leakage actually catch?** A target-derived `customer_devices`
field and same-timestamp peer leakage were both found and fixed, with tests added
`[docs/what_went_wrong.md]`.

## 5. How did you build hard negatives, and why do they matter?

Hard negatives are legitimate transactions that resemble fraud — travel, payday,
shared devices, near-threshold amounts — simulated as explicit episodes
(`hard_negative_target_share` in config). They matter because a model that only
learns "unusual = fraud" fails on exactly the legitimate behaviour that a real
analyst is paged about. The simulator deliberately weakens trivial shortcuts: a
small share of legitimate transactions share devices and a configured share of fraud
reuses known devices, so "new device" is not a giveaway
`[config/config.yaml; docs/methodology.md]`. The false positives are reported by
hard-negative type in `final_results.json:fn_fp.fp_by_hard_negative`.

**Follow-up: did they hurt the headline metric?** They make the task harder and the
numbers more honest; the gate (below) measures whether the data became *too* easy
instead.

## 6. Why XGBoost; why keep Logistic Regression and rules?

XGBoost captures non-linear interactions and leads on PR-AUC
({{headline.model.xgb_ci}} vs LogReg {{headline.model.logreg_ci}}) and on the
per-scenario recalls `[final_results.json:model_comparison, per_scenario]`. Logistic
Regression is kept as an interpretable, well-calibrated reference — it is close on
ranking ({{headline.model.logreg_ci}}) and its coefficients are readable
`[results/logreg_coefficients.csv]`. Rules are a transparent baseline the business
already trusts; a strong rules baseline that the model beats is more convincing than
beating nothing. In production these three play different roles: rules for policy
speed, LogReg for explainability/vetoes, XGBoost for ranking.

**Follow-up: is LogReg good enough?** On this near-separable synthetic task it is
competitive, which is itself a finding about the simulator (Experiment 8C).

## 7. What did the imbalance experiment show about calibration?

`scale_pos_weight = neg/pos` was the best imbalance lever (8A), and it barely
distorted probabilities on this near-separable task. Random undersampling *hurt*
ranking and distorted the base rate, and sigmoid calibration only repaired the
distorted models — it did not improve an already-calibrated model
`[docs/experiments/8A_imbalance.md]`. The practical conclusion: fix the imbalance in
the loss, then check calibration rather than assuming weighting broke it.

**Follow-up: so is calibration unnecessary?** No — the amount-dependent decision
threshold is only valid on calibrated probabilities, so calibration is required even
when the ranking is fine `[final_results.json:calibration]`.

## 8. What does calibration do, and why is a raw probability not trustworthy?

Calibration maps the model's score to a probability that matches observed
frequencies. FraudLens fits a sigmoid on `val_thr` and reports Brier/ECE before and
after: raw → calibrated Brier {{calibration.xgb.brier_raw:.6f}} →
{{calibration.xgb.brier_calibrated:.6f}}, ECE {{calibration.xgb.ece_raw:.6f}} →
{{calibration.xgb.ece_calibrated:.6f}} `[final_results.json:calibration]`. A raw
score is not a risk number because the expected-cost decision uses `p` in a formula
(see Q9); if `p` is not calibrated, the threshold is wrong and the cost is wrong.
Ranking metrics do not care about calibration, but money does.

**Follow-up: how do you pick sigmoid vs isotonic?** By validation performance on
`val_thr`, as a hyperparameter; the frozen choice is stored in
`results/freeze.json:frozen_thresholds.selected_calibrator`.

## 9. How did you pick the threshold? Derive the amount-dependent threshold.

With only APPROVE vs REVIEW and a review catch rate c=1, intervening is worth it
when expected cost of REVIEW < expected cost of APPROVE:
`R + (1−p)·F < p·(α·A + fixed_fee)`, which rearranges to
`p > (R+F)/(α·A + fixed_fee + F)`. That is **amount-dependent**: large amounts get a
lower threshold because the fraud loss avoided is larger. FraudLens implements the
full four-action cost model and chooses the action with least expected cost; the
frozen global capacity threshold is
{{freeze.frozen_thresholds.cost_optimal_threshold_capacity}}
`[freeze.json:frozen_thresholds; risk/policy.py]`. Cost-optimal beat F1-optimal on
realized cost by {{headline.decision.reduction}}.

**Follow-up: should the percentage carry a confidence interval?** No — I report the
percentage as a point estimate and give the interval in INR, because the ratio's
denominator is itself resampled `[final_results.json:decision.meta.reduction_ci]`.

## 10. Why does review capacity matter, and how was it modeled?

Analysts are finite, so a policy that flags hundreds of cases a day is fiction.
FraudLens caps REVIEW cases per day ({{freeze.capacity_per_day}}/day here,
`[freeze.json:capacity_per_day]`) and allocates the cap by largest expected saving,
demoting the rest to the next-best action. On the test window the policy flags only
{{headline.decision.capacity}}, so the cap never bound — the result is the
*unconstrained* policy and is **not** evidence the cap works
`[final_results.json:decision.meta]`. That honesty matters: I report a non-binding
capacity rather than pretending to have exercised it.

**Follow-up: how would you test the cap?** Deliberately lower the cap in a
sensitivity run and confirm demotions appear in expected-saving order
(`tests/test_api.py` exercises the demotion path).

## 11. What happens under concept drift; covariate vs concept drift; retraining strategies?

Covariate drift is the inputs moving; concept drift is the input→label mapping
moving. In the drift lab the scenario mix changes mid-timeline. Naive adversarial
validation on all features looks dramatic, but that is almost entirely the monotonic
**tenure clock** — strip the four tenure features and behavioural adversarial AUC
drops to a mild level `[docs/experiments/8E_drift.md]`. Retraining recovers only a
few hundredths of PR-AUC, and `sliding_60d` matches `expanding` on a fraction of the
rows, which argues for a rolling window. For FraudLens' own dev window the drift line
is {{headline.drift.line}}.

**Follow-up: which retraining strategy wins?** Sliding-window here; recency
weighting under-performed because soft weights still mix stale rows
`[docs/experiments/8E_drift.md]`.

## 12. Did the Isolation Forest help on unseen fraud? Why or why not?

No — and I report it as a null result. The Part 09 experiment asked whether an
unsupervised anomaly detector adds recall on a fraud pattern the supervised model
never saw; the bagged S1−S0 and S1s−S0 confidence intervals include 0
(`{{freeze.anomaly.s1_minus_s0_bagged}}` in `[freeze.json:anomaly]`), and the
detector's gain depended on having seen the pattern unlabeled. So the decision is
`adopted = {{freeze.anomaly.adopted}}` and no anomaly artifact was saved. Reporting a
clean null is more valuable than dressing up a coin flip.

**Follow-up: would you try it again?** Yes, with labels for a *related* pattern and
a proper temporal backtest `[docs/experiments/09_unseen_fraud.md]`.

## 13. What do SHAP values mean and not mean? How do importance methods differ?

SHAP values are additive pushes on the model's **raw margin** (log-odds):
`base_value + Σ SHAP = margin`, verified to ~1e-5 `[tests/test_explain.py]`. They are
**not** points on the 0–100 risk score — the sigmoid calibrator is non-linear, so a
"+0.6 SHAP" is not "0.6 risk points". `mean|SHAP|` measures **usage**, not utility: a
feature can dominate it while contributing nothing out-of-sample, so it is reported
beside permutation PR-AUC drop, and the two disagree on the tail where gain and
permutation correctly flag planted control columns
`[results/shap_global_dev.csv; docs/experiments/8D_feature_importance.md]`.

**Follow-up: which method do you trust?** For utility, permutation drop; for
per-transaction explanation, SHAP — but always alongside the calibrated probability.

## 14. What did the fairness audit find, and what are its limits on synthetic data?

The audit reports per-group recall, FPR and calibration for the frozen policy, with
small cells suppressed (n_pos<30 or n<1000; on test {{fairness.suppression[0].n_groups}}
age groups reported, {{fairness.suppression[0].suppressed_positives}} suppressed)
`[final_results.json:fairness.suppression]`. Intervals are cluster bootstraps over
customers. The limit is fundamental: the data is synthetic, so any group gap is the
**simulator's assumption** about age/income/archetype, not demographic reality. The
audit is therefore a demonstration of *method*, not a compliance claim
`[docs/fairness_audit.md]`.

**Follow-up: could you deploy this?** No — real fairness testing requires real data
and governance.

## 16. How is training/serving skew prevented here?

Feature logic exists in exactly one place (`src/fraudlens/features/`), and the API
imports the same serving path rather than re-implementing it — so training and
serving cannot drift apart by construction. A test replays 220 validation
transactions through the live API and asserts the calibrated probability matches the
offline pipeline to 1e-6 `[tests/test_api.py:test_parity_matches_offline_pipeline]`.
Parity wording: {{headline.engineering.parity}}.

**Follow-up: postgres vs memory?** {{headline.engineering.parity}}
`[docs/api.md]`.

## 17. What did not work?

Several things, all logged in `docs/what_went_wrong.md`: the first simulator was
**too easy** (the separability gate failed — crude signals solved it), so the
simulator was made harder and the gate fixed. A `tiny` calibration/policy run once
**overwrote the dev model's calibrator and policy** because both profiles mapped to
the same version string; a profile-guard test now blocks that. The IEEE R5
amount-aware policy initially used the raw, uncalibrated score — fixed and re-run,
both runs logged. A concurrency benchmark showed a single Python process does not
scale (GIL + XGBoost), so the latency headline is a concurrency-1 in-process number
with a lower-bound caveat. And the 83.23% cost reduction has **no CI for the ratio**
— reported as a point estimate with an INR interval instead.

**Follow-up: which was the most damaging?** The same-version clobbering, because it
silently corrupted a frozen artifact; the fix was a hard profile assertion plus a
test.

## 18. What are the limitations of synthetic fraud data?

The simulator encodes my beliefs about behaviour and fraud scenarios, so the model
learns *those*, not the world; the drift is a scenario-**mix** change rather than a
true concept shift. Hard negatives and device sharing are approximations. There is
no network/graph signal and no investigator feedback loop. Every subgroup result is
a statement about the simulator. That is why the project reports a null plus a
sim-to-real experiment instead of claiming transfer `[docs/limitations.md]`.

**Follow-up: what would fix it?** Real data with chargeback labels, graph features
and a proper temporal backtest.

## 19. What would you change before a real deployment?

Add real device/network/graph signals and late-arriving chargeback labels; build an
investigator feedback loop and a model-risk governance process; validate fairness on
real data; replace the in-process latency estimate with a load-tested service; add
data-drift and label-drift alerts with an on-call retraining runbook; and make
capacity allocation persistent (the current cap only sees the request batch). None
of these exist here, which is exactly why this is documented as a portfolio system
`[docs/limitations.md; docs/retraining_plan.md]`.

**Follow-up: what is deployable today?** Nothing as-is; the API and app are demos.

## 20. How would you retrain and monitor in production?

Monitor per-period score PSI, feature PSI, fraud prevalence and delayed-label
recall; trigger retraining with pre-stated rules — score PSI > 0.25 for two
consecutive periods, or recall at the frozen policy below a floor
`[docs/retraining_plan.md; src/fraudlens/monitoring/metrics.py::retraining_trigger]`.
The rollover evidence comes from Experiment 8E (rolling window beats unbounded
accumulation). Retraining would be a scheduled job with a champion/challenger
comparison and a freeze-style approval before promotion.

**Follow-up: what does the current monitoring show?** Per-month tables over the
train+validation timeline; the test-window PSI is quoted from
`results/final_results.json` and is not recomputed
`[results/monitoring_dev.csv]`.

## 60-second pitch

FraudLens is an end-to-end fraud-risk system on synthetic data. A behavioural
simulator feeds point-in-time features into Rules, LogReg and XGBoost, all validated
temporally with an embargo timed to the label delay. The frozen XGBoost test PR-AUC
is {{headline.model.xgb_ci}} at {{headline.model.test_prevalence_pct}} prevalence,
and an amount-aware, capacity-aware cost policy cut realized fraud cost
{{headline.decision.reduction}} versus the F1-optimal threshold
`[final_results.json]`. Drift is reported honestly — {{headline.drift.line}} — and
the isolation-forest experiment is a documented null. It ships with a FastAPI
service, a Streamlit analyst console, leakage and parity tests, and figures
regenerated from results.

## 2-minute pitch

Start with the problem: fraud is rare, adversarial and non-stationary, so accuracy
is meaningless and a random split lies. FraudLens simulates transactions and hard
negatives, computes point-in-time features with a leakage suite that includes a
canary, and compares Rules, LogReg and XGBoost under a chronological split with an
embargo equal to the label delay. XGBoost leads ({{headline.model.xgb_ci}} vs
LogReg {{headline.model.logreg_ci}} and Rules {{headline.model.rules_ci}})
`[final_results.json:model_comparison]`. Calibration is fit on the validation
window, then an amount-dependent cost policy with an analyst-capacity cap decides
APPROVE/STEP_UP/REVIEW/BLOCK; it reduced realized cost by
{{headline.decision.reduction}} `[final_results.json:decision]`. On the test window
{{headline.decision.capacity}} — the cap never bound, which I state plainly rather
than claiming capacity works. Drift is a first-class finding:
{{headline.drift.line}}, with the drift switch ({{headline.drift.switch_date}}) and
the rise of structuring as hypotheses, not established facts. The unsupervised
anomaly experiment is a null. The system serves through FastAPI with a parity test
and a Streamlit console for analysts, and the headline numbers are rendered from
`results/`, never typed by hand.
